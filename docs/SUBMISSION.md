## Testing instructions for judges

1. On a Quest, open **https://d2p3027seoreck.cloudfront.net/?fast** in Meta Quest Browser and tap **Enter VR** (or **In my room** for passthrough).
   No install and no account; hands or controllers both work. Sit down: the layout is designed for seated use.
   `?fast` makes timers run in seconds instead of minutes (Focus 25 lasts 25 seconds), so you can see what the agent
   does when a block ends. Without it, everything runs in real minutes.
2. A short guided start shows three steps: pick a card up, drop it in *Doing*, start *Focus 25*. It takes under a
   minute, or you can tap **Skip**.
3. Tap **Plan my day** on the left panel. The agent proposes a plan on a card in front of you, each step with its
   reason. Tap **Apply**. Before entering, you can also type or dictate your day in the *Describe your day* box on
   the page.
4. Work by hand: tap or pinch a card to pick it up, then tap a column to drop it. Grab a board's bar to move it
   anywhere.
5. When the focus timer ends, the agent suggests the next block on its own (or a break, if the task is not
   finished). One tap accepts it.
6. **AI log** shows every action the agent took, with the reason and an **Undo** for each step.
7. **Talk** (top right of the left panel): say a command ("focus", "undo", "yes", "high contrast on") or a request
   in your own words ("move the dentist call to the top and give me a short break"); the agent answers with a plan
   to review, and "yes" applies it. The browser asks for the microphone the first time.
8. **Hands-free** (bottom of the left panel): look at any control and hold your gaze until the ring fills to press
   it. Together with Talk, the whole workspace works without hands or controllers.
9. **In my room** (Quest 3/3S passthrough): the workspace opens in your real room. Tap **Fit room** and the board you
   work on stands on your real desk while the other boards hang on your walls. The room remembers it: next time you
   enter, every board is back in its place, even if you reorganised in VR meanwhile.
10. When a focus block ends, the **focus plant** on your desk grows a new leaf (every sixth block, a blossom).
11. **Wrap up** (or say "call it a day"): the agent names what you finished, clears it away and puts tomorrow's first
   task on top. Tomorrow's start-of-day plan picks up from its words. After 17:00 it offers this on its own.
12. **Contrast**, **Sound** and **Read aloud** are on the left panel. **Bring to me** gathers everything around you.
   The top of the panel shows today's focus blocks, your streak and the last seven days.

On a headset with a narrower view (Meta VR Glasses, or any Quest with Meta's field-of-view simulation on), the layout
fits itself to the view the headset reports, and you can look at a control and pinch to press it.

Without a Quest, open https://d2p3027seoreck.cloudfront.net/?emulate&fast in Chrome or Edge on a desktop. Meta's
IWER emulator stands in for the headset. Click **Enter VR**, then click the panels with the mouse.

The AI runs live on Amazon Bedrock. Each session can ask for 20 plans an hour. If the AI is ever unavailable, a
built-in planner answers instead and says so on the card.

## About the project

### Inspiration

A headset can surround you with more screens than any desk, yet most productivity in VR is still flat windows
floating in space, with a chat box bolted on. We wanted the opposite: a workspace that is spatial by design, and an
AI that works *on* that space rather than talking about it, without ever taking control away from the person
wearing it.

### What it does

You sit down, put the headset on and describe your day: "Finish the quarterly report, review two pull requests,
call the dentist, gym at 18:00." SpatialDesk turns that into a workspace around you. A *Today* board sits at arm's
length, the rest of your boards wait at the sides, and a 25-minute focus timer starts on the most important task.

- **The agent acts on the space.** Its answers are plans made of real operations: create a board, move or edit a
  card, start a timer, arrange the space. Each step comes with a reason, and nothing changes until you tap
  *Apply*.
- **It notices things on its own.** When a focus block ends, it suggests the next one, or a break if the task is not
  finished. When a task runs over its estimate, it offers to replan. When a board gets crowded, it offers to
  regroup it. Each suggestion waits for one tap.
- **Everything is explained and reversible.** The *AI log* lists every action with its reason and an *Undo* per
  step. Undo works even after you have changed things since. If a later step depended on the one you undo, that
  goes too, and the app tells you.
- **Not a wrapper.** Take the AI away and SpatialDesk is still a complete workspace: boards, timers, hands-first
  interaction, undo, and a built-in planner that answers when the AI cannot.
- **Talk to it.** Press *Talk* and say a command ("focus", "undo", "yes") or a request in your own words ("move the
  dentist call to the top and give me a short break"). Requests come back as a plan to review; "yes" applies it.
- **No hands needed at all.** In *hands-free* mode you look at a control and hold your gaze until a ring fills; where
  the headset tracks the eyes it follows them, so the head can stay still. Reading never presses anything: a control
  waits for the gaze to leave before it can be pressed again, and after a press nothing happens until the gaze moves.
  With *Talk*, the whole workspace works without hands or controllers.
- **Ready for Meta VR Glasses.** The app measures the view the headset renders and fits the layout into its
  comfortable part: on a Quest as designed, on the glasses the panels shrink to 0.85 so the board, its timer and the
  agent's card stay inside Meta's 58 x 54 degree perceived view (the glasses' sharper display keeps the text as
  legible). Look and pinch works on every control, and hover follows Meta's eye-tracking guidance: a subtle colour
  change on the control itself, never an outline.
- **In your room, remembered.** *In my room* opens the workspace in passthrough. *Fit room* reads the planes the
  headset detected: the board you work on stands on your real desk at arm's length with its timer and the focus plant
  beside it, and the parked boards hang on your real walls, clear of doors and windows. A persistent anchor keeps the
  layout in the room, so tomorrow every board is back on your desk and walls, even after reorganising in VR. Tested in
  all 13 rooms of Meta's Synthetic Environment Module, none of them tuned for.
- **A studio to work in.** In VR you work at dusk, at a walnut desk the board stands on, over low hills on the
  horizon. Every cue is one soft instrument, heard from where it happens, and a quiet ambience breathes in during a
  focus block.
- **A reason to come back tomorrow.** A focus plant on your desk grows a leaf with every focus block and blossoms
  every sixth; it never wilts, a day off just waits. Days have a start and an end: in the morning the agent offers to
  plan today from what is still open; in the evening, to wrap the day up, naming what got done and putting tomorrow's
  first task on top, and the next morning starts from its words. The command panel shows a streak and the last seven
  days.
- **Hands first, seated, accessible.**
  - Every target is at least 6 cm and within reach.
  - The board you work on sits 62 cm away, slightly below the eyes.
  - Looking at a side panel to tap it never moves your desk.
  - There is a guided start of under a minute, high contrast, spatial sound cues and read-aloud plans.

### How we built it

- **Client:** Meta's Immersive Web SDK (TypeScript, three.js, UIKit panels), delivered as WebXR so it opens from a
  link. The workspace is stored in IndexedDB and works offline.
- **One engine, two runtimes:** the workspace rules (boards, cards, timers, the seated layout and every operation)
  are a Kotlin Multiplatform module. The headset runs it compiled to JavaScript, and the server runs the same code on
  the JVM.
- **The agent:** Spring Boot calls Amazon Nova 2 Lite on Bedrock with one forced tool, `submit_plan`, whose steps are
  the engine's own operations. Before the headset sees a plan, the server:
  1. fixes the model's usual slips;
  2. checks guardrails computed from the workspace (a task at 25 of 120 minutes is not done; a replan starts no
     timers);
  3. validates the plan on the engine;
  4. retries once with the exact error.

  If that still fails, a built-in planner answers, and the card says so.
- **Voice:** an AudioWorklet turns the microphone into 16 kHz PCM, framed in AWS's event stream format and streamed
  straight to Amazon Transcribe over a WebSocket that the server presigns (SigV4, checked against AWS's documented
  example). The browser never holds AWS credentials and no audio passes through our server.
- **Mixed reality:** an immersive-ar session with plane detection and anchors (IWSDK's SceneUnderstandingSystem).
  The fit is pure math on the detected planes (desk at desk height in front of you; walls hit by the direction each
  parked board already had), and poses are stored relative to a persistent anchor, restored with
  `restorePersistentAnchor` next session. Tested end to end in IWER with Meta's Synthetic Environment Module rooms.
- **Hands-free and gaze:** the gaze ray (the eyes where tracked, else the head) is tested against each panel's plane,
  then against the control rectangles on the one looked at; a one-draw-call shader ring shows the dwell. No allocation
  per frame. Look and pinch is IWSDK's gaze pointer driving the same panels as the hands.
- **Field of view:** when a session starts the app reads each eye's projection matrix and takes Meta's perceived share
  of it (58/74 across, 54/68 vertically, from the glasses' published values). The shared engine finds the largest
  panel scale at which the board, its timer and the agent's card fit, and refits what sits in its slot.
- **The studio:** a procedural desk, plant and horizon, one unlit draw call each with the light baked into their
  vertex colours, measured within noise of the scene without them; the plant rewrites its preallocated geometry in
  place while a leaf unfurls.
- **AWS, all in CDK:**
  - S3 and CloudFront serve the app.
  - A load balancer reachable only from CloudFront fronts the server on Fargate.
  - The server's role may call that one model and stream to Transcribe, nothing else.
  - Quotas keep the cost of the whole competition bounded.
- **Tested end to end in Meta's IWER Quest 3 emulator with Playwright.** Every quality claim is an automated check:
  - frame budgets on a CPU throttled 3x, held no heavier than Meta's own IWSDK starter scene;
  - hit rates under hand-tracking jitter;
  - seated layout invariants;
  - a guided start of under a minute;
  - gaze presses, glances that must not press, and the no-repeat rules, with the head as the only input;
  - a spoken command (synthesised, played as the browser's microphone) heard by the real Amazon Transcribe and
    turned into a plan;
  - a plan applied in under 4 seconds against production.

  The demo video is a recorded run of that same test suite, with a locally generated voice-over and captions.

### Challenges we ran into

- **Building for a headset without one.** We turned every comfort and performance claim into a test that fails the
  build. The frame budget is set against Meta's own starter scene measured on the same machine, so "it should run
  fine on a Quest" is not a guess.
- **Hand-sized UI that stays fast.** Listening for pointer events on every button made the framework raycast every
  element on every frame (9 fps). One listener per panel with a rectangle hit test brought the app's own cost under
  half a millisecond per frame. The hands-free gaze first cost 2.6 ms; testing every third frame against control
  rectangles cached in each panel's frame brought it to 1.3 ms.
- **An agent that is right, not just fluent.** Nova 2 Lite kept marking unfinished work as done when a timer
  ended. A longer prompt did not fix it. The server now computes facts ("25 of 120 minutes used: not finished") and
  rejects plans that contradict them, feeding the reason back for one retry.
- **Undoing one step out of order.** Undo is a journal of operations replayed with their original clock and
  position. Any single step can be removed later, and anything that depended on it is reported.
- **Gaze that does not press everything you read.** A plain dwell timer picked up every card you read and pressed
  whatever was behind a panel that had just closed. Two rules fixed it: a pressed control waits for the gaze to
  leave, and after any press nothing is pressed until the head moves.
- **Voice in a headset browser.** The browser's own speech recognition is missing from some browsers and could not be
  verified in Meta Quest Browser, so the app streams raw audio to Amazon Transcribe itself.
- **Seated reality.** You look at the panel you are tapping. Our first version re-anchored the workspace to wherever
  the head pointed, so a tap on the side panel swung the whole desk 50°. A head-turning test caught it, and the desk
  now keeps its heading through glances.

- **Designing for a field of view we cannot see.** No Meta VR Glasses here, so the app never assumes a device: it
  reads the projection the headset reports, and a test checks every corner of the essential panels against the
  glasses' perceived view in IWER's glasses profile.
- **Rooms we never saw.** Running *Fit room* in all 13 of Meta's synthetic rooms found three real faults: a long desk
  that was missed, timers standing off a small table's edge, and a board hung over a window. Each is now a rule and
  a test.

### Accomplishments that we're proud of

- An agent you can audit: every action has a reason and its own undo.
- Every control has four ways in: hands (poke or pinch), look and pinch, voice, and the eyes alone.
- Ready for Meta VR Glasses before they ship: the layout fits the view the headset reports, verified in IWER's glasses
  profile.
- The same engine validates the AI on the server and runs the workspace in the headset.
- Quality numbers instead of adjectives, measured in the emulator on the production build:
  - 1.3 ms per frame (p95) for the app's own systems, measured with hands-free on, its heaviest mode;
  - 76 draw calls and a 2.26 MB bundle;
  - a hit rate of 95 % or more under jitter;
  - plans in about 2 seconds.

### What we learned

- Agents become trustworthy when their output is a structured plan the app can check, show and undo, not text.
- Facts computed by the app ground the model better than longer prompts.
- With WebXR and IWER, a full XR product can be designed, tested and recorded from a desktop.

## How hand interactions are implemented (optional form field)

Everything is built for hands first, with no controller needed at any point:

- **Pick and place instead of drag.** Tap or pinch a card to pick it up; every column lights up as a drop target; tap
  one to drop it. No step needs more than 2 cm of precision, so tracking jitter never drops a card halfway.
- **Poke and pinch both work everywhere.** Each panel is one IWSDK `PokeInteractable` and `RayInteractable`; the
  control under the finger or the ray is found with a rectangle test, which keeps the app's own cost under half a
  millisecond per frame.
- **Sized and placed for real hands, seated.** Targets are at least 6 cm with 2 cm gaps, the working board sits 62 cm
  away just below the eyes, and an automated test injects ±1 cm / ±3° of hand-tracking noise and requires a 95 % hit
  rate.
- **Grab bars, not grabbable panels.** Boards move by a bar under them (one-hand grab or a distant pinch), so a pinch
  on a card is always a tap and never drags the board by accident.
- **And when hands are not an option:** every control also works by voice and by gaze alone (hands-free).

What excites us: hands, voice and gaze together make a headset usable by people a keyboard and mouse never served
well, and an agent that acts on the space turns "talk to your desk" into something you can check and undo.
